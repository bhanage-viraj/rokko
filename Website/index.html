Once upon a time…

…there was a child who learned best by seeing, touching and doing, and a grown-up who wanted to share chess with them.

Every day…

…chess looked like the wrong way in.

📱

Rules on a screen

Most chess apps are fast, loud and played alone. They teach with words and arrows, not stories and hands.

🧭

Hard to find help

Chess tutors who understand autistic children are expensive, or simply not nearby.

🤷

"I don't play chess"

Many grown-ups would love to share chess, but don't know how to teach it themselves.

One day…

…they opened Rokko, and a story began.

Inside Rokko is a story of its own: a jungle where six animals become the six chess pieces. It's the first of three ways your child learns, in every lesson.

1

A story to see

A short film gives every chess piece a character and a reason to exist. Pictures and a story come first, so the names and rules have something to hold on to.

  • Captions match every spoken word
  • Each piece appears from its story, one at a time
  • Watch again whenever you like
Because of that…

…the story turned into things to touch and things to hold.

2

Games to touch

Small games your child taps and drags: find the piece, match it, build a team. There's one thing on screen at a time, the same question every time, and spoken help when it's needed.

  • No scores, no buzzers, no red
  • A try that doesn't fit gently sinks back
  • Help comes as a clue, then a pointer, then the answer
3

A board to hold

Between games, the grown-up hands over the real piece. Your child holds it, feels its shape and puts it on a real chessboard. The lesson ends where chess belongs: the two of you at the board.

  • Clear steps for the grown-up on every turn
  • No chess knowledge needed to lead
  • Uses the chess set you already have
And because of that…

…Rokko learned their pace, too.

No two children learn the same way. Rokko watches how each game goes and quietly adjusts, so your child stays in the zone where learning feels good.

🌱

Their pace

Games get a little harder only when your child is ready, one small step at a time, and only between games, never in the middle.

🤝

Help that fades

Stuck? A clue appears, then a pointer, then the answer. As your child grows more sure, the help steps back.

🌤️

Their day

Having a wobbly day? The grown-up says so, and Rokko eases off: simpler games, calmer pictures, or a break.

Inside the app

Calm, clear and predictable.

The frame never moves. Every step waits for the grown-up to tap Next. Calm mode softens pictures and sound, and Rokko follows the iPad's Reduce Motion setting.

🔒

Nothing leaves your iPad

Rokko has no accounts, no ads, no tracking and no internet connection at all. Your child's progress is kept only on your iPad.

Until finally…

…they sat side by side, at a real board.

That's why it's called Rokko.

Rokko comes from castling, the only move in chess where two pieces move together in a single turn. The king steps toward the rook, and the rook crosses over to stand beside him and keep him safe.

Russian players call it rokirovka, Italians arrocco. Rokko is drawn from those words.

That's exactly how Rokko works: the child is the king, the grown-up is the rook, and they move together, side by side.

The Rokko logo is castling. Green rook: the grown-up. Gold king: the child.
